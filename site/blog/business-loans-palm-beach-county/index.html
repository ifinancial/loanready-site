If you run a business in North Palm Beach, Palm Beach Gardens, Jupiter, West Palm Beach or up the Treasure Coast, you have more financing options than ever, and fewer easy approvals. Banks are cautious, short-term funders have tightened up, and lenders can see far more about you than they used to.

This guide walks through the main types of business loans, what every lender looks at, the documents to have ready, and the strategy that separates owners who get approved from owners who collect declines.

The main types of business loans

Picking the right product is half the battle. Each one fits a different need:

  • SBA 7(a) loans: long terms and competitive rates for working capital, equipment, expansion, buying a business and more. Up to $5 million, with more paperwork and stricter rules than most loans.
  • SBA 504 loans: long-term, fixed-rate financing for owner-occupied commercial real estate and major equipment.
  • Bank term loans: a fixed amount repaid on a fixed schedule, often used to refinance expensive debt or fund a specific project.
  • Business lines of credit: revolving working capital you draw on when you need it and pay down when cash comes in.
  • Equipment financing: loans or leases where the equipment is the collateral, often with simpler approval.
  • Commercial real estate loans: to buy, refinance or build property, from banks, SBA 504 lenders or private lenders.
  • Asset-based lending: borrowing against receivables, inventory or equipment, sized by what your business owns.
  • Short-term and same-day funding: fast, but expensive. Useful as a bridge, dangerous as a habit.

We break down each one in our business loan guides, including what lenders check for that specific product.

Which loan fits your situation

  • "My cash is tight between jobs or seasons." A line of credit, sized to your working capital cycle.
  • "I need a truck, a machine or new equipment." Equipment financing, where the equipment secures the loan.
  • "I'm buying my building." SBA 504 or a commercial mortgage, usually with 10–25% down.
  • "I'm buying another business." Often SBA 7(a), with at least 10% equity injection.
  • "I'm stuck in daily payments." A conventional term loan or bridge to retire the advances first, not another advance.
  • "I need money this week." Short-term funding can work, but plan your exit before you sign.

What every lender looks at

Product details vary, but almost every underwriter is answering the same questions:

  • Can you repay? Documented cash flow compared to your total debt payments, known as debt service coverage. Many lenders want 1.25x or better.
  • Will you repay? Personal credit for each significant owner, payment history, recent inquiries and existing debt.
  • How stable is the business? Time in business, revenue trend, customer concentration and industry.
  • What's behind the loan? Collateral, a down payment or equity injection, and personal guarantees.
  • Do the documents agree? Your tax returns, financial statements, bank statements and credit report should tell the same story.

That last point is where most declines start. A strong business with books that don't match its tax returns, or a good credit score next to bank statements full of payments to cash advance companies, looks risky on paper.

Lending has changed: why strategy matters now

A few years ago, applying everywhere and taking the best offer was a reasonable approach. Today it backfires:

  • Lenders can see where else you applied. Hard inquiries show up on your personal and business credit, and business loan inquiries generally don't get the rate-shopping protection that mortgages and auto loans do.
  • Cash advances leave a trail. Most file UCC liens, and the daily or weekly payments show on your bank statements.
  • Short-term funders share data with each other and have tightened their standards. Many now look for credit in the 620s and up, more documents, and stricter limits on stacking.
  • SBA rules tightened in 2025, including a higher minimum credit score for its streamlined small loans and a ban on using SBA proceeds to pay off merchant cash advances.

So the question isn't just "will someone approve me?" It's where you apply, when, in what order, and with what file. We explain this in more detail in how lending has changed.

Documents to have ready

Requirements vary, but a complete file for a bank or SBA loan usually includes:

  • Two to three years of business and personal tax returns
  • A year-to-date profit and loss statement and balance sheet
  • Three to twelve months of business bank statements
  • A debt schedule listing every loan, line, lease and advance
  • A personal financial statement for each owner with 20% or more
  • Business formation documents, licenses and your EIN
  • For specific purposes: equipment quotes, a purchase contract, a lease or projections

Having this ready before you apply makes you look organized and serious, and it shortens the time from application to funding.

Five mistakes that get Palm Beach County owners declined

  • Applying with five lenders at once. Five inquiries, five chances to be told no, and every lender can see the others.
  • Applying right after a bad month. Lenders review recent bank statements. Timing your application after strong deposits can change the answer.
  • Stacking cash advances to cover payments. Each new position makes your statements look worse to a bank.
  • Tax returns that show too little income. Write-offs save taxes but can sink your debt coverage. See write-offs vs. loan approval.
  • Ignoring personal credit. For most small businesses, the owner's personal credit is still central to the decision.

Local resources worth knowing

Palm Beach County has solid support for small businesses, including the Business Development Board of Palm Beach County, county and city loan programs, SCORE volunteer mentors, and the Florida Small Business Development Center network. Frequently asked questions

What credit score do I need for a business loan?

It depends on the loan. Many banks prefer personal scores in the high 600s or above, SBA lenders often look for 680 or higher, and short-term funders have raised their minimums too. The rest of your file, including cash flow and documentation, matters as much as the score.

How long does it take to get a business loan?

Short-term funding can close in days. Bank and SBA loans usually take several weeks to a few months, depending on how complete your file is when you apply.

Can I get a business loan with bad credit?

Sometimes, but usually at a higher cost. The better path is often to fix the file first, then apply for financing that costs less. We can help with both.
