Grow Your Business

How to grow your business without choking your cash flow

Growth eats cash. Here's how to fund it so you scale up without trading your margins to expensive money.

Growth is the goal, and it's also one of the most common reasons good businesses run out of cash. You land a bigger contract, hire ahead of demand, buy inventory for the busy season or open a second location, and suddenly money is going out months before it comes back in.

The owners who grow well aren't just good at selling. They fund growth deliberately. This guide covers the principles we use with clients across Palm Beach County and the Treasure Coast to plan growth that doesn't choke cash flow.

Why growth eats cash

Most businesses pay for growth before they get paid for it. A contractor buys materials and pays crews weeks before the draw arrives. A distributor stocks inventory months before it sells. A medical practice hires staff before the patient base fills their schedules. That gap between cash out and cash in is your working capital need, and it grows as you grow.

When owners don't plan for it, they fill the gap with whatever is fastest, usually credit cards or a merchant cash advance. Fast money is expensive money, and expensive money eats the margin that was supposed to fund the next step.

Rule 1: Match the money to its purpose

The most common funding mistake is financing long-term assets with short-term money. A simple guide:

  • Seasonal swings and working capital: a business line of credit. Draw when you need it, pay it down when receivables come in.
  • Equipment and vehicles: equipment financing with a term that roughly matches the equipment's useful life.
  • Expansion, acquisitions and refinancing expensive debt: a term loan, often SBA 7(a) for longer terms.
  • Real estate: long-term commercial mortgages or SBA 504.
  • A true emergency or a fast opportunity: short-term funding as a bridge, with a planned exit into cheaper money.

When the term of the money matches the life of what it pays for, the investment pays for itself instead of draining daily cash. You can compare payments for each type in our loan calculator.

Rule 2: Build banking relationships before you need them

The best time to meet a banker is when you don't need money. Where you bank, and how you bank, quietly shapes what you can borrow later:

  • Choose a bank that lends to businesses like yours, not just one with a convenient branch. Community banks and credit unions in Palm Beach County often have more flexibility than national banks for local owners.
  • Keep your deposits there. Your deposit history is one of the best underwriting tools a bank has. Splitting revenue across five accounts makes your business harder to read.
  • Get to know a relationship manager and send them your financials once a year, even when you're not asking for anything.
  • Keep the account clean: no NSFs, no negative days, no personal spending.

Owners who do this get treated as clients. Owners who show up only when they're desperate get treated as applications.

Rule 3: Watch the numbers lenders watch

You don't need a finance degree, but you should know these numbers every month:

  • Gross margin: what's left from each sale after direct costs. If growth lowers your margin, it may not be worth it.
  • Debt service coverage: cash flow compared to debt payments. Many lenders want 1.25x or better.
  • Days sales outstanding: how long customers take to pay. Faster collections are the cheapest financing there is.
  • Cash runway: how many weeks you could operate on cash on hand.
  • Customer concentration: if one customer is more than a quarter of revenue, lenders will notice, and so should you.

Current monthly books make all of this visible. Without them, you're steering by feel. Our bookkeeping and tax team builds these reports every month.

Rule 4: Plan your next two rounds, not just this one

Every financing decision affects the next one. Taking a high-cost advance today may solve this month and block the bank line you'll need next year. Over-writing off expenses saves taxes in April and shrinks the loan you qualify for in June.

We map growth funding 12 to 24 months ahead. What will you need, when, and for what? Which lender fits each step? What does the file need to look like by then? Then every move, from how you file taxes to when you apply, supports the plan.

Rule 5: Build credit capacity in the business

A strong business credit profile, separate from your personal credit, gives you more ways to fund growth: vendor terms, business cards and lines that don't depend entirely on your personal score. With the right credit profile and financial footprint, some owners also qualify for 0% introductory-rate business credit options, which can be a powerful, low-cost tool when used with a payoff plan. Signs you're ready to grow, and signs to wait

You're likely ready if: your gross margin is stable or rising, your books are current, you have at least a few weeks of cash runway, demand is consistent rather than one-time, and your debt payments are comfortably covered by cash flow.

Consider waiting, or fixing the foundation first, if: you're covering payroll with advances, your books are months behind, one customer makes up most of your revenue, or your personal credit has slipped in the last year. Growth on a shaky foundation magnifies the cracks. Fixing them first usually takes a few months, and it lets you grow with cheaper money when you do.

What well-funded growth looks like

Ragtop is a good example. The business had real demand but was buried in merchant cash advances and high-interest debt. Rather than add another advance, we built a plan: six loans over six months, each one cheaper than the money it replaced. Every MCA was paid off, and with cash flow freed up, the owner opened a new division that now does more than $2 million a year.

That's the difference between being funded and being financed well.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best way to fund business growth?

Match the financing to the purpose: lines of credit for working capital, equipment financing for equipment, term or SBA loans for expansion, and long-term mortgages for real estate. Avoid funding long-term growth with short-term, high-cost money.

When should I talk to a bank about a loan?

Before you need one. Build the relationship early, keep your deposits and financials with them, and share your plans. You'll get better terms and faster answers when the time comes.
