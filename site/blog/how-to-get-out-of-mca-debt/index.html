MCA Debt

How to get out of merchant cash advance debt without defaulting

How MCAs really work, what they truly cost, and a step-by-step way out that protects your business and your credit.

Almost every owner stuck in merchant cash advances tells a version of the same story. One advance covered a slow month. The daily payments made the next month tighter, so a second advance covered that. Then a third. Now a big share of every day's deposits goes out the door before payroll, rent or inventory.

If that's where you are, you're not alone, and you're not out of options. This guide explains how MCAs actually work, what they really cost, and the realistic steps out, without defaulting and without making things worse.

How a merchant cash advance works

A merchant cash advance usually isn't a loan. Legally, it's typically structured as the purchase of a portion of your future receivables. The funder gives you a lump sum, and you agree to repay a larger fixed amount, usually through daily or weekly automatic withdrawals.

  • Factor rate: instead of an interest rate, MCAs use a factor, often between about 1.1 and 1.5. A $100,000 advance at a 1.499 factor means you repay $149,900.
  • Holdback or fixed payment: a set daily or weekly debit, or a percentage of sales.
  • UCC filing: most funders file a lien on your receivables, which other lenders can see.
  • Reconciliation clause: many MCA contracts let you request an adjustment if your actual sales drop, because the payment is supposed to reflect a share of receivables.

What an MCA really costs

Factor rates look small because they aren't annualized. Take that $100,000 advance at 1.499, repaid over about 12 months of business days. You pay back $149,900, so the cost of the money is $49,900. Because you start repaying the next day, the effective annual cost works out to roughly 87%, by our estimate.

Compare that to an 11% APR bank loan for the same amount and term: total repayment is about $106,000. The MCA costs about $43,800 more for exactly the same money. You can plug in your own numbers in our MCA vs. bank loan calculator.

That gap is why getting out matters. It's money that could be going to payroll, growth, or your own pocket.

Step 1: Stop the stacking

The single most important move is to stop adding positions. Every new advance makes your bank statements look worse to a bank, adds another UCC filing, and increases the daily drain. It feels like a solution, and it's the thing that keeps the trap closed.

Next, get organized. List every advance: funder, original amount, payback amount, balance remaining, payment frequency and amount, and contract terms. You can't build a plan without the full picture.

Step 2: Use the contract, not just the phone

Read your agreements. If they include a reconciliation provision and your sales have genuinely dropped, you may be entitled to request a payment adjustment based on actual receivables. Do it in writing, with documentation.

Many funders will also discuss a restructure, such as a longer term or lower payment, if you're proactive and transparent, especially before you miss payments. A restructure without default protects your relationships and avoids the escalation that follows a missed payment.

Step 3: Be careful with "MCA debt relief" companies

Some debt settlement companies tell owners to stop paying their funders while they negotiate. That can trigger a default, collection actions, frozen accounts, and a UCC lien that makes future bank financing far harder. Some arrangements work out, but go in with your eyes open, read the contract, and understand the fees before you sign anything. Our approach is different: restructure without default where possible, then refinance into lower-cost financing.

Step 4: Refinance with lower-cost conventional financing

The real exit is replacing expensive money with cheaper money: a bridge loan, a conventional term loan or a line of credit that pays off one or more advances and gives you a single, lower monthly payment.

One important 2025 change: SBA loan proceeds can no longer be used to refinance merchant cash advances. That's why the first refinance is usually conventional financing, with SBA coming later for growth once the advances are gone and your file is clean.

To qualify for that refinance, lenders will look at your credit, your bank statements over the past several months, and whether your books and taxes show the cash flow to support the new payment. Which brings us to the next step.

Step 5: Fix the footprint banks will review

While you're stabilizing payments, start making your file bankable:

  • Credit: dispute inaccurate items, bring utilization down and avoid new inquiries. See our credit repair program.
  • Bank statements: reduce NSFs and negative days, and keep business and personal spending separate.
  • Books and taxes: current financial statements that tie to your returns, and a tax plan that shows the income a lender needs. See bookkeeping and tax planning.

Questions to ask before you take another advance

  • What's the total payback amount, not just the factor rate?
  • What will my combined daily and weekly payments be across all positions?
  • Does this contract include a reconciliation provision, and how do I use it?
  • What happens if my sales drop, and what counts as a default?
  • Will this advance pay off another one, or stack on top of it?
  • How will this look on my bank statements when I apply for a bank loan in six months?

If the answers make you uneasy, call us before you sign. A different structure often costs less.

What the path out looks like

  • Today: a bridge loan, or restructuring current advances without a default, to get relief.
  • Months 1–3: fix the footprint, with credit, books, statements and a tax plan. No more stacking.
  • Months 3–6: lines of credit and term loans that pay off the expensive money.
  • Months 6–12: long-term bank and SBA financing for growth.

It works. Ragtop came to us buried in MCAs and high-interest debt. Over six months we arranged six loans, paid off every MCA, and the owner opened a new division now doing more than $2 million a year. Burning Hearts Tattoo had two MCAs. After credit repair, they paid both off early and saved on the payback.

Frequently asked questions

Can I refinance an MCA with an SBA loan?

Not under current rules. Since 2025, SBA loan proceeds can't be used to pay off merchant cash advances. Conventional term loans, lines of credit or bridge loans are usually the first refinance step.

What happens if I stop paying my MCA?

It can trigger a default under your contract, collection actions, possible account freezes and enforcement of the funder's UCC lien. It also makes future bank financing much harder. Talk to us, and to an attorney, before you stop paying.

How long does it take to get out of MCA debt?

It depends on how many positions you have and your cash flow. Many of our clients follow a 6–12 month path: stabilize and fix the file first, refinance into lower-cost money, then move to bank or SBA financing.
