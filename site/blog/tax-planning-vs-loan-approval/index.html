Bookkeeping & Tax

Write-offs vs. loan approval: tax planning for business owners who want to borrow

Saving on taxes and qualifying for a loan pull in opposite directions. Here's how lenders read your returns, and how to plan for both.

Here's a conversation we have almost every week. An owner runs a healthy business: $900,000 in revenue, steady customers, a growing team. They apply for a loan and get declined. The reason? On paper, the business barely made any money.

Nothing illegal happened. The owner (or their tax preparer) did what most owners are told to do: write off everything possible. That saved taxes. It also told the bank that the business can't afford a loan payment.

This guide explains how lenders read your financials, why write-offs and borrowing power pull against each other, and how proper bookkeeping and tax planning let you have a reasonable amount of both.

How lenders calculate your income

Banks and SBA lenders don't lend against revenue. They lend against documented cash flow, and the main document is your tax return. A typical underwriter starts with net income from your business return, then adds back certain non-cash or one-time items:

  • Depreciation and amortization: these reduce taxable income but aren't cash leaving the business.
  • Interest on debt that the new loan will replace.
  • Some clearly one-time expenses, if they're well documented.

What usually does not get added back: personal expenses run through the business, a family vehicle, vague "consulting" fees and cash you can't document. Those reduce your income in the lender's eyes, just as they did on your tax return.

Debt service coverage: the number that decides your loan

Once a lender has your cash flow, they compare it to your total annual debt payments, including the new loan. This is your debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). Many lenders look for 1.25x or better, meaning $1.25 of cash flow for every $1.00 of debt payments.

Let's run the example. Say the new loan payment is $5,000 a month, or $60,000 a year.

  • Aggressive write-offs: net income on the return is $40,000. Add back $15,000 of depreciation and $5,000 of interest, and cash flow is $60,000. DSCR is 1.0x. That's a decline.
  • Planned for the loan: the same business, with personal expenses kept out of the books and owner compensation structured deliberately, shows $75,000 of net income. With the same add-backs, cash flow is $95,000. DSCR is about 1.58x. That's an approvable file.

The business didn't change. The paperwork did. Yes, showing more income can mean paying more tax. But compare that cost to the difference between bank money and expensive short-term funding, and the math usually isn't close.

Why books that don't match your returns stall underwriting

Lenders ask for your tax returns and your year-to-date financial statements. If the profit and loss statement shows $200,000 of profit and the tax return shows $30,000, the underwriter has to figure out which one is true. They'll usually assume the lower number, or stop and ask questions that delay your file for weeks.

Common causes include books kept on a different accounting method than the return, year-end adjustments that never made it back into the books, missing depreciation, and personal and business spending mixed in the same account. Clean, current bookkeeping that ties to your returns is one of the strongest signals you can send a lender.

Timing matters as much as the numbers

When you file and when you apply can change your answer:

  • Extensions: if you extend your return, lenders may rely on the prior year plus interim statements. If last year was your best year, filing sooner may help.
  • Year-to-date performance: a strong first half with clean interim financials can support a file even when a prior year was soft.
  • Big purchases: large equipment write-offs (such as Section 179 expensing) can sharply reduce taxable income in the year you buy. That can be great tax planning, or a problem if you need a loan next spring. Plan it on purpose.

Questions to ask your tax preparer before you file

If you plan to borrow in the next 12 to 18 months, ask these before your return is final:

  • What net income will this return show, and what will a lender's cash flow calculation look like?
  • Which deductions are cash expenses, and which are non-cash items a lender may add back?
  • Are any personal expenses running through the business that we should stop?
  • Should we take a large equipment deduction this year, or spread it out?
  • Does my owner compensation make sense for both taxes and lending?
  • Will my year-end books match this return exactly?
  • Should I file now or extend, given when I plan to apply?

A preparer focused only on minimizing this year's tax bill may give different answers than one who also understands lending. Both perspectives matter, and the best plan weighs them together.

Owner pay and entity structure

How you pay yourself affects what the lender sees. In an S corporation, for example, the IRS expects a reasonable salary, and the rest of the profit may flow through as distributions. Lenders look at both the business and the owner's personal return, and each structure has trade-offs. This is exactly where a CPA who understands lending earns their fee. The right answer depends on your situation, so treat this as background, not advice.

What lender-ready books look like

  • A separate business bank account and card, used only for business
  • Monthly reconciliations, with every account matching the bank
  • A monthly profit and loss statement and balance sheet you can produce on request
  • Year-end books that tie to the filed tax return
  • A current debt schedule listing every loan, line and advance
  • A personal financial statement for each owner that matches their credit report

Frequently asked questions

Do lenders use my tax returns or my bank statements?

Banks and SBA lenders rely primarily on your tax returns and financial statements. Bank statements matter too, especially for lines of credit and short-term lenders, and underwriters will look for NSFs and payments to other funders.

What is a good DSCR for a business loan?

Many lenders look for 1.25x or higher, meaning $1.25 of cash flow for every $1.00 of annual debt payments. Requirements vary by lender and loan type.

Will showing more income mean paying more taxes?

Possibly. The goal of planning is to find the right balance for your goals: legally minimizing taxes while still showing the income you need to qualify. Often the savings from bank-rate financing outweigh the extra tax.
